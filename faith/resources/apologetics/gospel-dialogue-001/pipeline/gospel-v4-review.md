# Gospel v4 Draft Review

---

## 1. Does the structure climax at the cross?

Yes. Part 3, Section 14 ("The Cross: The Climax") is the structural peak of the document. Every prior section builds an element of the legal case that the cross section then assembles and resolves. The sequence is disciplined:

- Part 1 establishes the legal problem (Creator's authority, original law, the fall, the wages of sin, the courtroom roles).
- Part 2 establishes that no existing solution resolves the problem (works, other worldviews, the need for an outside solution).
- Part 3 builds to the cross: Trinity/incarnation, the nature of love, then the cross as the point where everything converges.
- Part 4 delivers the verdict (resurrection) and the response.

The cross section in the draft opens with: "This is where the entire case converges." It then recaps each element of the established case before showing how the cross answers each one. This is the correct structure.

Verdict: structure holds. The cross is the climax.

---

## 2. Is the tone factual and logical throughout, or did storytelling creep back in?

The tone is substantially improved from v3. The narrative opener from v3 ("Imagine you are watching the second half of a film...") has been removed. The majority of the document is declarative, direct, and analytical.

### Sentences requiring correction or watchlist

The following sentences move toward devotional or atmospheric language and should be revised in the final letter:

**Section 2 — "No barrier. No fear. No hiding."**
Reads like a three-beat poetic phrase. Acceptable as a summary of the biblical description, but should be stated as a factual summary rather than a rhetorical rhythm.
- Suggested revision: "There was no barrier between Creator and creature, no fear, no separation."

**Section 2 — "This is what was lost. And this is what the gospel is about recovering."**
The sentence "this is what the gospel is about recovering" is close to the v3 phrasing and carries a mild narrative warmth. Not a serious problem, but can be made more precise.
- Suggested revision: "The gospel is the account of how what was lost was recovered."

**Section 13 — "The life he has becomes available to the one who is in him."**
Slightly abstract and almost devotional in phrasing. Clarify with a more precise legal statement.
- Suggested revision: "The standing and life that belong to Him become the standing and life of those who are in Him."

**Section 13 (love section) overall:**
The love section serves its purpose — defining love as sacrifice rather than feeling. However, the transition from the love section into the cross section should make clear that the love section is a definitional preparation, not an emotional warm-up. Add one sentence before the cross section that explicitly connects: the cross is the event in which this love is legally expressed.

**Section 14, cross section — "Into that situation, Jesus steps."**
"Steps" is a slightly dramatic word. Acceptable — it is not narrative in the story sense — but could be made more clinical.
- Suggested revision: "Into that situation, Jesus enters as substitute."

No instances of "Imagine...", "Think about...", "Picture...", or "Like watching..." were found in the draft. The prohibition is honored.

## 3. Are the four key questions answered?

### Why is God who He is?

Yes. Addressed in Section 1 (God Is Creator) and Section 6 (Holy, Righteous, and Just). His authority is sourced from creation, not arbitrary power. His holiness, righteousness, and justice are grounded in His nature and explained with Scripture. The "who made God?" question is addressed compactly and correctly.

### Why do we need the gospel?

Yes. Addressed through Sections 3-8: the fall, the legal nature of sin, God's holiness and justice, the courtroom, the wages of sin. The reader is brought step by step to the point where guilt is established, the standard is clear, and the impossibility of self-resolution is stated.

### Why couldn't it be any other way?

Partially. Section 11 (the solution must come from outside) addresses this — a guilty party cannot pay their own debt and survive. Section 9 addresses why good works don't work. Section 12 addresses why the substitute must be both human and divine.

However, the argument for "why blood?" is present in Section 8 but could be stated more explicitly in the cross section itself. The final letter should include one direct sentence in the cross section that states: this is why it required blood — because life is the currency of the debt, and a bloodless atonement would be a debtless atonement.

### Why is the gospel the best explanation of morality, of all worldviews, of the state of the world today?

Addressed in Section 10 (Why Other Worldviews Fail the Diagnosis). Each major worldview is treated compactly and the shared failure identified: all put the burden on the guilty party to pay a debt they cannot pay. The gospel correctly diagnoses guilt as a legal debt and provides an outside solution.

One gap: the draft does not explicitly state that the gospel is the only worldview that makes justice and love simultaneously coherent. The cross is where justice is satisfied (not set aside) and love is demonstrated (not merely declared). This should be stated explicitly once, either at the end of Part 2 or at the opening of Part 3.

## 4. Does the reader understand the price was paid for them personally?

Yes, addressed in Section 18: "Whosoever. That is a specific offer to a specific person: you." The "whosoever" language is correctly used to make the universal offer personal.

The cross section itself uses "the iniquity of us all" (Isaiah 53:6) and "a ransom for all" (1 Timothy 2:6). The personal application is made explicitly in the closing section.

One improvement for the final letter: the call to action in Section 18 currently says "pray honestly." This is correct but brief. Add one sentence that names the personal nature of the debt more directly — something like: "The charge that was pressed against you specifically was answered at the cross specifically. The question is whether you will receive that answer."

## 5. Are analogies brief and clarity-only?

Yes, with one watchlist item.

- Creator-authority analogy (manufacturer/architect): one paragraph, explains the concept, moves on. Correct.
- Tree of Life analogy (immortality-while-poisoned): present in Section 5. This analogy runs slightly long in the draft. In the final letter, trim the analogy to two sentences: state the parallel, map it to the biblical situation, proceed.
- Crime-vs-charity analogy (good works): one paragraph, correctly clinical. Correct.
- Legal-debt-currency argument (can't pay with same currency): stated as logic, not as an analogy with illustrative language. Correct.
- Burning building parallel (faith): introduced with "Here is a parallel:" — correctly flagged, clearly stated, moves on. Correct.
- Zechariah 3 vision: this is biblical imagery from Scripture itself, not an imposed analogy. Kept in full as Scripture. Correct.

No analogies linger past their purpose in the draft, with the Tree of Life section being the borderline case.

## 7. Are there sections that interrupt the logical flow?

The hard questions (who made God, why evil, can we trust the Bible) are embedded as subsections within the relevant parts of the main argument. This is correct and follows the plan.

One structural flag: the "Can we trust the Bible?" section at the end of the draft (appended to Section 18) feels like a footnote rather than part of the argument. In the final letter, this should either:
- Be placed at the opening as a brief statement about the nature of the sources being cited, or
- Be moved to its natural position before the call to action in Section 18, with a clear transition: "One more question must be answered before the call to respond."

The worldviews section (Part 2, Section 10) is in the correct position — before the cross, as part of the case for why the solution must come from outside. It does not interrupt flow.
