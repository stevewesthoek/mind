I appreciate the strongest version of your objection, and I think it deserves a careful reply. Atheism, strictly speaking, is often just a lack of belief in gods. Science is a method, not a religion. And some of my earlier wording moved too quickly from "not yet explained" to "therefore God."

I agree with that criticism in part.

Where I think the deeper disagreement remains is not over atheism as a label by itself, but over the worldview that sometimes sits alongside it: metaphysical naturalism, the assumption that reality is ultimately impersonal, and the view that reality is ultimately explainable in impersonal natural terms.

I am not claiming that atheism and Christianity are the same kind of thing. I am claiming that atheism, by itself, is not meant to answer all the larger questions we are discussing. Once the conversation moves into the nature of reality, reason, morality, consciousness, and meaning, the deeper question becomes whether naturalism is sufficient.

That is a philosophical question, not a scientific slogan.

Science is a reliable way of studying the physical world. It is not blind faith. But science depends on things it does not itself establish: the reliability of reason, the validity of logic, the uniformity of nature, and the trustworthiness of our observations. Those are not laboratory findings. They are preconditions for doing science at all. That does not prove theism by itself, but it does show that science rests on deeper philosophical foundations.

One of the hardest questions for strict materialism is why conscious, rational, first-person awareness exists at all.

It is also true that consciousness is closely linked to the brain. Brain injury, drugs, sleep, and disease all affect thought and awareness. That is a real and important fact, and it should not be minimized.

What remains open is whether impersonal matter is enough to explain not just brain function, but subjective awareness, rational reflection, and meaning. That is a philosophical question that is still live, not a scientific fact settled against materialism.

If morality is only social preference or evolutionary convenience, then moral language becomes hard to ground. But most people do not merely say "I dislike cruelty." They mean cruelty is really wrong.

That experience fits more naturally with moral realism than with a worldview where moral claims are only preferences or survival strategies. That does not settle the question by itself, but it is a serious reason to think moral experience points beyond reductionistic materialism.

It is important not to confuse abiogenesis with evolution. Evolution explains how life diversifies once life exists. It does not explain the first living system.

Likewise, fine-tuning is not proof that God exists. It is an inference: the universe appears life-permitting in a striking way, and design is one possible explanation. That is a philosophical argument about the best explanation, not a scientific proof.

If I make a Christian case, I should do it the way the New Testament teaches: with gentleness and respect, not hostility or mockery; with patience, not quarrelsomeness; and with an eye toward truth, not scoring points.

That is the posture in 1 Peter 3:15-16 and 2 Timothy 2:24-26. It is also consistent with Paul’s approach in Acts 17:22-31, where he reasons publicly with a non-Christian audience while also calling people to repent and turn to the living God.

And if I speak about the created order, Romans 1:18-25 and Psalm 19:1-4 fit best as theological framing: creation bears witness to God, but Scripture is not asking me to overclaim scientific certainty or to turn every unresolved question into an argument from ignorance.

The resurrection should be treated as a cumulative historical argument, not as a slogan. The relevant question is not whether history can be repeated in a laboratory. The question is which explanation best accounts for the historical data often discussed in resurrection studies: Jesus’ crucifixion, the early resurrection proclamation, the rise of the earliest Christian testimony summarized in 1 Corinthians 15:3-8, and the claims made by his followers very early in the movement.

That is a historical judgment, not a scientific measurement, and it should be argued that way.

I would not say that atheism is a religion in the same sense as Christianity, that all atheists are committed to scientism, that consciousness has not been shown to arise from physical processes at all, that science tells us the universe came from nothing, that transitional fossils are absent, that entropy makes evolution impossible, or that breeding only removes information. Those claims are too broad or simply incorrect.

I do think atheism, when it becomes naturalism, still has serious questions to answer: why reason tracks reality, why objective moral obligation feels real, why consciousness exists, why the universe is intelligible and life-permitting, and why the historical case for Jesus deserves a serious hearing.

That is why I think theism remains a serious and coherent explanation.

Not a certainty imposed by force. Not a shortcut around evidence. A serious explanation that deserves to be considered on its own terms.
